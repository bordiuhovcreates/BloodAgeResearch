Two people born in the same year rarely age at the same speed. One may keep the energy, recovery and metabolic health of someone a decade younger, while another shows early signs of wear long before any diagnosis appears. Chronological age counts the years. The pace of aging describes what those years have actually done to the body.

Blood is one of the most practical ways to study that difference. A single sample carries signals from almost every organ system, and when the same markers are measured repeatedly, they start to show not only where a person is today, but how quickly things are changing. In this article we explain what blood biomarkers are, why repeated measurements matter so much, and which signals tell us the most about how fast the body ages.

## What a biomarker actually measures

A biomarker is any measurable value that reflects a biological process. In blood, this includes familiar values such as glucose, cholesterol and blood cell counts, as well as less routine signals: inflammatory proteins, hormones, markers of kidney and liver function, and molecules linked to how cells repair themselves.

![Blood samples in labelled test tubes](/images/article-biomarkers-910.webp "Every tube is labelled and tracked, so each value can be linked back to the same person and the same moment in time.")

On their own, most of these values say little about aging. A slightly raised marker can follow a poor night’s sleep, a recent infection or a heavy training session. What matters is the pattern: how several markers move together, and whether that movement continues over months and years.

It also helps to remember that a biomarker is an indirect signal. Albumin, for example, is a protein made by the liver; its level reflects nutrition, liver function and inflammation at the same time. Creatinine reflects both kidney filtration and muscle mass. Reading these values well means understanding everything that can move them — not only aging.

## From snapshots to trajectories

A one-time blood test is a snapshot. It is useful, but it cannot separate a temporary fluctuation from a lasting shift. This is why our research focuses on repeated measurements taken under the same standardized conditions — the same time of day, the same preparation and the same laboratory methods.

> A single value tells us where the body is. A series of values tells us where it is heading.

When measurements are collected this way, small but consistent changes become visible. A gradual rise in inflammatory signals, a slow decline in kidney filtration or a drift in metabolic markers can appear long before any of them crosses a clinical threshold.

This is an important distinction. Clinical reference ranges are designed to detect disease: a value is either inside the range or outside it. Aging research looks at movement inside the range. A marker that stays “normal” but rises steadily every year may be more informative than one that is briefly out of range once.

## How a sample becomes data

Behind every value on a report is a chain of steps, and each step can introduce variation. To keep long-term data comparable, we follow the same protocol for every participant and every visit:

1. **Preparation.** Samples are taken in the morning after an overnight fast, with no intense exercise the day before.
2. **Collection.** Blood is drawn into standardized tubes, labelled with a unique code and logged with the exact time.
3. **Processing.** Tubes are spun in a centrifuge within a fixed time window to separate plasma and serum from cells.
4. **Analysis.** Markers are measured with validated laboratory methods, and quality controls run alongside every batch.
5. **Storage.** Part of each sample is frozen, so new markers can be measured later in the same material.

![Blood tube being placed into a laboratory centrifuge](/images/article-biomarkers-2-910.webp "Processing every sample within the same time window keeps values from different visits comparable.")

These steps are not glamorous, but they decide whether a trend in the data is biology or simply a change in how samples were handled.

## Markers we follow most closely

Our panels combine routine clinical tests with research markers. Among the signals that carry the most information about the pace of aging are:

- **inflammation markers** such as C-reactive protein, which reflect chronic, low-grade inflammation;
- **glucose regulation**, including fasting glucose and HbA1c, which shows average blood sugar over the past two to three months;
- **kidney function**, estimated from creatinine and related markers, and its slow change over the years;
- **liver and nutrition markers**, including albumin and liver enzymes;
- **blood cell composition**, such as the share of lymphocytes and the variation in red blood cell size;
- **lipid profiles** and markers of vascular health.

None of these markers is a verdict on its own. Together, they form a profile that can be compared across time and across thousands of people of similar age.

## What changes the pace

The pace of aging is not fixed. Studies consistently link it to factors that are, at least partly, within reach: sleep, physical activity, diet, smoking, chronic stress and the management of existing conditions. Genetics plays a role as well, but it explains only part of the difference between people.

For research, this is what makes the pace of aging so interesting. If a profile changes faster than expected, the next question is why — and whether that change can be slowed. Repeated blood measurements allow us to follow such changes in real time, instead of waiting years for clinical outcomes.

## Why the pace matters

Understanding the pace of aging changes the question researchers ask. Instead of waiting for disease to appear, we can study the early, quiet phase when biological systems begin to lose resilience — and learn which patterns predict that loss.

For research, this opens a way to test whether lifestyle, environment or future interventions can slow the process, and to measure the result in the blood rather than years later in clinical outcomes. It is long-term work, but each well-documented sample brings the picture of human aging into sharper focus.

## Key takeaways

- Chronological age counts years; the pace of aging describes how fast the body actually changes.
- Single blood tests are snapshots — repeated, standardized measurements reveal trajectories.
- Movement inside normal ranges can be more informative for aging research than a single abnormal value.
- Careful sample handling is what makes long-term trends trustworthy.
- No single marker defines aging; the combination and its change over time do.
