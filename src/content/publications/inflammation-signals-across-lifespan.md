Inflammation is one of the body’s most important tools. It helps fight infection, heal wounds and clear damaged cells. But with age, the system that switches inflammation on and off becomes less precise. Researchers often describe the result as “inflammaging” — a low, persistent level of inflammation that lingers even when there is no obvious threat.

This background inflammation is usually silent. It does not cause fever or pain, and it can stay unnoticed for years. Yet it appears in the blood, and it is closely linked to many of the changes that define biological aging. In this article we look at how inflammation changes across life, how it can be measured, and why the timing of these signals matters as much as their level.

## A system built for short responses

In younger bodies, an inflammatory response tends to rise quickly, do its job and fade. The immune system then returns to a calm baseline. Over time, several factors make that return slower and less complete: accumulated cellular damage, changes in immune cell populations, metabolic stress and shifts in the gut microbiome.

![Researcher examining a sample under a microscope](/images/article-inflammation-910.webp "Laboratory analysis helps separate short inflammatory spikes from a slow, persistent rise.")

The outcome is not a single dramatic event, but a gradual change in balance. Signals that once appeared only during illness start to remain slightly elevated in everyday life.

## Where persistent inflammation comes from

There is no single source of inflammaging. Research points to several processes that add up over the years:

- **Senescent cells.** Cells that stop dividing but do not die can release inflammatory molecules into their surroundings.
- **Immune aging.** The balance of immune cells shifts with age, and the system becomes both less effective against new threats and more prone to background activation.
- **Metabolic stress.** Excess visceral fat, high blood sugar and insulin resistance are associated with higher inflammatory signals.
- **Barrier changes.** Changes in the gut lining and microbiome can expose the immune system to more microbial fragments.
- **Lifestyle factors.** Poor sleep, chronic stress, smoking and physical inactivity are all linked to higher baseline inflammation.

These sources overlap and reinforce each other, which is one reason inflammation is so closely tied to aging as a whole.

## What we see in the blood

Inflammation can be traced through several groups of markers. Some are proteins produced by the liver in response to immune signals, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and fibrinogen. Others are messenger molecules released by immune cells themselves, such as interleukin-6. Blood cell counts add another layer, showing how the proportions of different immune cells change — for example, the ratio of neutrophils to lymphocytes.

Looking at these markers together allows us to distinguish between:

- short-term spikes after infection, injury or intense exercise;
- recurring patterns linked to stress, poor sleep or recovery problems;
- slow, steady increases that persist across several measurements.

The third pattern is the one most closely connected to aging, and it is only visible when samples are collected repeatedly over time.

> Inflammation becomes meaningful for aging research not when it is high, but when it stops coming back down.

## Why one measurement is not enough

Inflammatory markers are among the most variable values in the blood. A common cold, a dental problem or a hard workout two days earlier can raise CRP several times above a person’s usual level. If such a value is read in isolation, it can easily be mistaken for a long-term change.

![Scientist preparing a slide at a microscope](/images/article-inflammation-2-910.webp "Repeated measurements help separate a person’s usual baseline from temporary spikes.")

To avoid this, our protocol records recent illness, medication and exercise at every visit, and inflammatory markers are always interpreted against a person’s own history. A value that is high once and then returns to baseline tells a very different story from one that rises slowly over several years.

## How inflammation connects to other systems

Inflammatory signals rarely act alone. They interact with glucose regulation, vascular health, muscle maintenance and the brain. That is why our research does not study inflammation in isolation, but alongside metabolic, kidney and cardiovascular markers.

When these signals rise together, they often point to a broader loss of resilience — the body’s ability to recover from everyday challenges. Understanding which combinations appear first, and in which order, is one of the central questions of our work.

## Looking ahead

Inflammation is not something to eliminate; the body needs it. The goal of research is to understand when the balance shifts, how early that shift can be measured in the blood, and which factors help restore it. Studies of sleep, physical activity, diet and new therapies all depend on reliable ways to measure this balance over time.

Every long-term profile we collect adds evidence to that picture — and brings us closer to understanding how a protective response turns into a quiet driver of aging.

## Key takeaways

- Inflammaging is a low, persistent level of inflammation that grows more common with age.
- It has many sources, from senescent cells to metabolic stress and lifestyle factors.
- Markers such as CRP, interleukin-6 and immune cell ratios trace it in the blood.
- Single values are easily distorted by short-term events; trends over time are what matter.
- Inflammation is studied together with other systems, because it rarely changes alone.
