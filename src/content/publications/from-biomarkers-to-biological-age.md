Most of us measure age in birthdays. Biology does not. Two 60-year-olds can differ widely in how well their heart, kidneys, metabolism and immune system work. Biological age is an attempt to describe that difference with numbers — to estimate how “old” the body behaves, rather than how long it has existed.

Blood biomarkers are one of the most promising foundations for such an estimate. In this article we explain what biological age means, how it can be calculated from routine blood values, and what such a number can and cannot tell us.

## What biological age means

Biological age is not a single measurement. It is a model built from many markers that change predictably over the lifespan. By learning how these markers typically look at different ages, researchers can compare an individual profile with population patterns and estimate where it fits.

If a person’s markers resemble those of people younger than them, their biological age may be lower than their chronological age. If the markers resemble an older group, it may be higher. The difference between the two is sometimes called “age acceleration”.

## Different clocks, different questions

Blood biomarkers are not the only way to estimate biological age. Researchers also use so-called epigenetic clocks, which read chemical marks on DNA, as well as measures based on proteins, physical performance or imaging. Each approach captures a different side of aging.

Clocks built from routine blood chemistry have a practical advantage: the markers are widely available, inexpensive and well understood. Several published models combine around ten common values — such as albumin, creatinine, glucose, C-reactive protein, the share of lymphocytes and the variation in red blood cell size — into one estimate that predicts health outcomes better than chronological age alone.

## Building a model from blood

Creating a reliable biological age estimate requires large, well-documented datasets. Our approach combines several steps:

- collecting standardized blood samples across a wide age range;
- selecting markers that show consistent links with age and health outcomes;
- training models that weigh each marker according to the information it carries;
- testing the models on new data to make sure they work beyond the original group.

Each step is designed to keep the model transparent, so researchers can see which markers drive an estimate and why.

> Biological age is most useful not as a score, but as a way to see which systems are aging faster than others.

## Beyond a single number

A single biological age value is easy to communicate, but it can hide important details. Two people with the same overall estimate may have very different profiles — one with strong metabolic health but elevated inflammation, another with the opposite pattern.

That is why we also study system-level estimates: separate views of metabolic, immune, kidney and cardiovascular aging. These help researchers understand where change begins and how it spreads between systems.

## Can biological age change?

Unlike chronological age, biological age is not expected to move in only one direction. Because it is calculated from markers that respond to health and lifestyle, the estimate can improve or worsen over time. Studies suggest that factors such as physical activity, sleep, diet, weight and the control of chronic conditions are reflected in many of the underlying markers.

![Smiling older woman hiking on a misty trail](/images/article-biological-age-2-910.webp "Because it is built from living signals, biological age can shift over the years — in both directions.")

This makes biological age a promising tool for research into interventions: instead of waiting decades for outcomes, studies can track whether the estimate changes over months or a few years. Any such change, however, needs careful interpretation and confirmation over repeated measurements.

## Limits and responsibility

Biological age models are research tools. They depend on the data used to build them, they carry uncertainty, and they should not be read as a diagnosis or a prediction of lifespan. A model trained on one population may not work equally well for another, and a single estimate can be affected by short-term events such as an infection.

Used carefully, however, they offer a clear way to describe aging, to compare groups and to measure whether future interventions make a real difference. Every new profile helps refine these models and brings us closer to understanding how — and how fast — the human body ages.

## Key takeaways

- Biological age estimates how old the body behaves, not how many years it has lived.
- Routine blood markers can be combined into clocks that predict health better than age alone.
- System-level estimates show which parts of the body are aging faster.
- Biological age can change in both directions, which makes it useful for research.
- Estimates carry uncertainty and are research tools, not diagnoses.
